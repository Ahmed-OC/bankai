# Design Engineering: A State of Mind (desengs) — the role is a mindset, not an ownership claim

Source: https://blog.designeng.club/p/design-engineering-a-state-of-mind — Andy Zhang, "Design Engineering - a State of Mind" (29 June 2025), the launch post of the Design Engineer Newsletter Series on the Design Engineering Substack (run by the Design Eng Club; site branding by cindy, website by mathurah). Zhang is a design engineer at Windsurf with ~6 years split across engineering (Google, pre-launch Figma 2016), design (Figma, Quora), and PM (Uber). Companion source: https://designeng.club/ — the club's own site ("A room for people who design and build"). No license; distill, don't reproduce.

## What it is / Why it exists

A first-person origin story that lands on a redefinition. As a teenage pianist in a string orchestra, Zhang watched the keyboard's power wire get severed before a concert; a cellist's father repaired it by biting into the plug to reconnect the wires and taping it with material scavenged from an unused percussion instrument. Asked how, the man answered "I'm an engineer." Engineering as fixing the wire for others — not a title, not a toolset.

The essay's tension: Zhang's zigzag career left him, in job searches, stuck "between the cracks of engineering, product and design" — repeatedly advancing deep into interviews, then rejected for lacking specialized tenure, despite being able to build great products. A rec-league basketball teammate saw the hybrid role as a feature; that week an interview, the next an offer, at Windsurf (then Codeium), a scrappy ~20-person team where the hybrid had full agency.

The redefinition that resolves the tension, verbatim: "The role evolved into a state of mind instead of state of ownership." The question that governs the work stopped being "which part of this feature is mine" and became "how can Windsurf bring as much delight to the user as possible?"

The evidence behind the redefinition: one career, three lenses. Each stint trained the perspective the mindset later rotates through.

- **Engineer**: software engineering at Waterloo — from a first Java program to scripts that located free campus food, overnight hackathons, a Google internship, and dev work for Hack the North; later engineering at Google and Figma.
- **Designer**: pure engineering felt disconnected from user impact, so he bought Sketch and made one sketch daily for 30 days, then spent two years building a portfolio — enough to land an engineering internship at pre-launch Figma (2016) and convert it into a formal design internship; later design at Quora.
- **PM**: design brought him closer to users but still didn't scratch the itch; product management at Uber, where he dogfooded Uber Eats as a courier in Amsterdam to feel the product from the rider's side.
- **All three at once**: sole engineer-designer at a YC startup whose product was acquired after three years — the hybrid role in miniature before it had a name that the market accepted.

Two transferable learning tactics hide in the biography: skill-switching through constrained daily practice (30 sketches in 30 days, not a course), and empathy through role immersion (courier, not a survey).

The club exists because that mindset now has enough practitioners to sustain infrastructure: a community, newsletter, and event series across SF, NYC, and online — "For design engineers, by design engineers" — that "Started from a tweet."

## The core ideas

### 1. Ownership is the wrong frame; delight is the objective function

State the goal as user delight on a surface, not possession of a layer. Consequences:

- No fix is out of scope because it belongs to another discipline. Zhang's solution space spans "copy changes, design tweaks, full facelifts, or blended design-plus-engineering work."
- The unit of accountability is the experienced problem, not the artifact (code, file, ticket) your role produces.
- Nothing in the flow is "not my job" — the mindset fills the gaps it sees, which is what "seeing through the cracks" means in practice.

### 2. One axis of change = local maxima

The essay's sharpest actionable rule, verbatim: "Limiting yourself to one axis of change can limit you to a local maxima." Rotating through engineer, designer, and PM perspectives is how you reach the global maximum. If every fix you ship comes from the same discipline's toolbox, you are optimizing inside a neighborhood, not the space.

Breadth is the design engineer's superpower: "seeing through the cracks, filling in details," and answering the who, what, and why — not just the how. The three lenses the essay's career implies:

- **Engineer**: how it works — cost, complexity, failure modes, feasibility of the fix.
- **Designer**: what it feels like — clarity, hierarchy, whether the confusion disappears.
- **PM**: who is hurt and why it matters — reach, evidence, urgency, whether this is even the right problem.

A fix is only "global" when all three lenses have spoken and the answer survived each.

### 3. Start backwards from the user's experience; tools are interchangeable means

Design engineers "needn't be tool experts"; they must bridge gaps, start backwards from the intended user experience, then select whatever tools realize the vision. The plug-biter was never trained to bite into a plug — the fix was simply a means to the end of delighting the user. Learning new tools on demand is a prerequisite, not a detour, because solutions are bespoke: no fixed recipe covers the next problem.

### 4. The daily practice is direct contact with signal

Zhang's stated routine: reading bug reports and internal/external feedback, studying data, and seeking elegant solutions "wherever they live on the stack." Insight comes from the raw material of user pain, not from the spec. The elegance criterion matters: among fixes that all work, prefer the one that is simplest at the point of experience, regardless of which craft produced it.

### 5. Why the mindset wins now: AI lowers the skill floor

Zhang attributes design engineering's rise to AI tooling and improved model intelligence lowering the skill floor — which makes breadth, amplified by AI execution, the decisive factor in reaching the best product outcomes. His own product, the Windsurf Editor (a VS Code fork whose agent understands the codebase and user intent, letting users "build 10x faster"), is the evidence: his day-one questions were improving failure modes of agent tool calls *through design*, compressing many features into tiny screen space, and crafting human-in-the-loop agent collaboration.

The corollary for teams: he calls combining professional depth as engineer, designer, and PM "a glimpse of future product teams." Domain expertise still matters — but its "impact ceiling is capped" without breadth.

His three day-one product questions, generalized — the most concrete design briefs the essay offers:

- **Failure-mode design**: improving how agent tool calls fail *through design* — every automated action gets an inspectable, correctable failure path.
- **Density compression**: compressing many features into tiny screen space — disclosure hierarchy as the primary tool, not smaller type.
- **Human-in-the-loop collaboration**: crafting where the human approves, corrects, or redirects the agent — the collaboration points are the product.

The community's shape is itself a reading of the field:

- **Lineage-building**: two separate General Magic events — a screening (at Notion HQ, with Design Film Club) and an "evening on mobile computing" Q&A with Marc Porat, Rich Miller, and Mike Stern, moderated by Soleio Cuervo. ## When to use / When NOT to use

Use when:

- Triaging any surface-level defect or friction: choose the axis of fix (copy / visual / interaction / structure / code) by user impact, before anyone claims the ticket.
- A UX problem falls between roles and stalls in handoff — the state-of-mind frame dissolves the ownership question by replacing it with an accountability-per-surface question.
- Building agentic or AI-assisted products: failure-mode design, feature compression, and human-in-the-loop are the essay's home turf.
- Deciding what to learn next: learn the tool the intended experience demands, not the one your label implies.
- Reasoning about team shape: where AI amplifies execution, hire and evaluate for breadth across who/what/why/how — depth alone is ceiling-capped.
- Auditing an existing team or agent workflow for its missing lens: if all recent decisions smell of one perspective (all "how", no "why"; all "feel", no cost), the next hire, reviewer, or checklist item is that lens.
- Shaping your own career narrative: the essay is proof that a winding path reads as liability in specialized hiring and as qualification under this frame.

Do NOT use when:

- The problem is genuinely single-axis and deep — pure performance, security, infra, or a hard accessibility audit wants a specialist's rigor; breadth does not substitute for depth (Zhang's breadth sits on years of depth in each discipline).
- You are inside an org whose role boundaries are load-bearing — the essay's conditions were a ~20-person team with full agency; process-heavy coordination needs explicit ownership, at least transitionally.
- "Mindset" becomes an excuse for shallow output in every craft — the essay is a claim about where to aim, not a waiver on craft standards.
- The user's problem is unvalidated — rotating lenses on the wrong who/what/why still lands in the wrong place; signal-reading (idea 4) precedes lens rotation.
- Speed of learning exceeds speed of validation — bespoke solutions assume you can absorb bug reports and data fast enough to steer; if you can't, narrow the surface first.

## How to apply

1. For each surface you touch, write the governing question in the essay's form: "how can [this product] bring as much delight to the user as possible?" If a proposed task can't be tied to that question, deprioritize it.
2. Before fixing any friction, enumerate the full solution ladder: microcopy/copy change → visual tweak → interaction change → layout restructure → full facelift → architecture change. Pick the lowest rung that fully removes the user's problem.
3. Rotate three lenses on every non-trivial decision and record what each sees before converging: PM (who is hurt, what evidence, why now), designer (clarity, hierarchy, feel), engineer (cost, failure modes, complexity). Converge on the global maximum, not the first workable fix.
4. Ingest raw signal directly: read bug reports, support feedback, and data yourself; never design purely from a translated spec.
5. Audit your last 10 shipped fixes. If they cluster on one axis, force the next equivalent problem through at least two other axes before choosing — that cluster is your local maxima.
6. On agent surfaces, design the failure path first: what the user sees and can correct when a tool call errors, times out, or does the wrong thing — a first-class design deliverable, not an error-toast afterthought.
7. Compress by progressive disclosure: when many features compete for small space, reveal in context instead of shrinking everything.
8. Keep a human in the loop as a structural constraint: design the collaboration points (approve, correct, redirect) into the flow from the start.
9. When a fix requires a tool you don't know, learn it at the moment of need; choose tools backwards from the intended experience (idea 3), and timebox the learning rather than avoiding the fix.
10. In team settings, assign problems to surfaces, not to disciplines: one accountable owner per user-visible problem, with license to pull whatever craft the fix needs.
11. Among equally-working fixes, prefer the one that is simplest at the point of experience — the essay's "elegant solutions" criterion — even when it is harder to build.
12. Borrow the club's demo discipline: be able to present any fix as a 3–5 minute story that starts from the user's problem, not the implementation — if you can't, the fix probably optimized the wrong thing.
13. When you must learn a new craft to unblock a fix, use constrained daily practice: one small artifact per day for 30 days, each publicly shareable — not a passive course.
14. When a fix's users are reachable, immerse before designing: use the product in the user's role for at least one full session (Zhang's courier run), then write down what surprised you before reading any analytics.
15. Gate lens rotation behind validation: confirm the who/what/why against at least one raw signal source (bug report, feedback thread, data cut) before generating fixes on any axis.

A minimal triage routine encoding rules 2–3 (framework-neutral):

```js
function triage(friction) {
  // Rule 2: the solution ladder, lowest rung first.
  const ladder = ["copy", "visual", "interaction", "layout", "facelift", "architecture"];
  // Rule 3: each lens can veto; the fix must survive all three.
  const lenses = { pm: seeWhoWhy, designer: seeClarityFeel, engineer: seeCostFailure };
  for (const rung of ladder) {
    const fix = draftFix(friction, rung);
    if (removesProblem(fix) && Object.values(lenses).every(seen => seen(fix) !== false))
      return fix;                       // global maximum for this friction
  }
}
```

The mechanism, not the syntax, is the point: ladder order fixes axis discipline; lens iteration fixes local maxima.

## AI-slop warning

How this essay gets misapplied. "State of mind" read as license to blur everything produces the redesign-where-a-sentence-would-do failure — the essay explicitly lists copy changes as a legitimate fix, so always run the solution ladder (step 2) before touching structure. "Breadth" read as skippable depth: an AI agent already has the breadth Zhang spent six years earning; its failure mode is the inverse of his — plausible-shaped fixes with no rigor on any axis. Guard: every lens rotation must be able to change the chosen fix, or it is theater; and each axis's output must still pass that craft's own bar (contrast ratios, motion timings, error handling), not merely resemble it.

Two more misreadings. The plug story read as "duct tape is fine": the improvisation was born of care for the concert (the user's outcome) and solved the problem completely — slop borrows the improvisation and drops the care, shipping quick fixes that half-solve. And "learning is a prerequisite" read as permanent tool-hopping: Zhang learned tools at the moment of need for a defined fix, not as procrastination — if the learning has no user problem attached, it is not the essay's lesson. Finally, do not copy the club's aesthetics (demo-night generative art, workshop vibes, member side-projects) as if they were the discipline — the events are discourse, not a style guide; the transferable content is the mindset and its themes (agentic interfaces, failure design, lineage, career paths).
